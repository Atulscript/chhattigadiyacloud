Editorial

Folk Idiom in Contemporary Scenography

Folk theatre in modern times: continuity, dance and scenography.

By Suresh Thakur

This fourteenth edition of Chhattisgadhiya Cloud Masik Patrika is dedicated to the living soil and evolving stage forms of Central India. Nacha, Panthi and Karma are not museum artefacts; they are kinetic, critical tools in dialogue with the modern world.

"Theatre remains invincible as long as its bare feet touch the living earth."

In these pages we travel from the village akhara to the proscenium, asking what the folk idiom gains — and what it risks — when it meets new audiences. We dedicate this issue to the grassroots torchbearers of regional oral performance, who kept the flame alive with little more than a dholak and a clearing under the sal trees.

Theatre Essay

The Revival of Nacha in Urban Spaces

How rustic satire and Panthi rhythms are captivating proscenium stages.

By Dr. Prabhat Mishra

Nacha has never been merely entertainment; it is the living courtroom of the village commoner. When the actors step into the circle, social barriers soften and the powerful become the easiest targets of laughter.

"The laughter generated in Nacha is not an escape from reality; it is a profound confrontation with reality itself."

In this edition we document the experiences of three generations of Nacha practitioners who have carried their repertoire into contemporary theatre halls while keeping the rural hearth glowing. Their testimony reveals a form that bends without breaking — shortening a night-long performance to ninety minutes, yet refusing to surrender its improvised heart.

Performance Analysis

Dialect, Cadence and Spontaneous Wit

The dholak's crescendo and the improviser's craft — Nacha, part two.

By Dr. Prabhat Mishra

What keeps Nacha modern is not its subject matter but its method. Three elements recur in every performance we recorded:

  1. Language of the earth — vernacular idiom that refuses formal stiffness and lands instantly with a local audience.
  2. Music as dramatic pulse — the dholak and manjeera drive each scene towards its comic crescendo.
  3. Spontaneous improvisation — punchlines rewritten in the moment, in response to the crowd and the day's news.

This organic elasticity is also its vulnerability. On a ticketed stage with fixed lights and a printed script, improvisation must be defended deliberately — or it quietly disappears.

Tribute

Remembering Habib Tanvir's Scenography

Space, Brecht and the bamboo screen.

By Kavita S. Joshi

Habib Tanvir stripped the proscenium arch of its Victorian clutter. Instead of heavy velvet and cardboard walls, he brought the open sky and simple bamboo frames.

His actors carried the set within their bodies. A step forward became a journey across a river; a sudden song opened the gates of an emperor's palace. The audience was trusted to complete the picture.

"Simplicity is the most formidable spectacle."

This organic synthesis — of folk performers, Brechtian distance and an almost empty stage — remains the guiding star of our own work at Chhattisgadhiya Cloud. On the next page, the archive opens his rehearsal notebooks.
